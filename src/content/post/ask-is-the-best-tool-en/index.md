What is the best tool an agent has? bash? read? write? I've always told the people around me that it's the `ask` tool.

Humans don't know what they want to do. With a feeble brain that can barely hold seven words in mind at once, we simply lack the capacity to define _what_ to build, let alone _how_ to build it in any concrete way. So what's left? An "impulse," or an "intent." Nothing but raw intent born from the direction of an emotion — something like "ugh, I want to fix this somehow," or "if I pull this off, people will love me."

And then all that remains is the grueling process of turning that raw impulse into a polished spec, a flowchart, some code. Usually this is the stage where you pull out a notepad, a notebook, a sheet of drawing paper, and start struggling. "God, it would be great if someone interviewed me and pulled out, one by one, the things I want but can't even name." Before LLMs, only a human could do this. But now, under the name of "agents," we have machines that extract what we want dozens of times a day.

In fact, this is exactly what every professional does. Ask the right questions to extract a client's own desires — desires the client isn't even aware of — then combine them with domain expertise and deliver an appropriate artifact. A lawyer produces documents, a doctor a prescription, a developer code. So if you simplify what an agent does to the extreme, it's a kind of translator. A translator that converts the language of raw intent into the language of executable output.

Seen this way, it also explains why professional classes are feeling such intense substitution pressure in the AI era. From an economic standpoint, a large share of a professional's added value was attached to precisely this "translation" step. The client doesn't know what they want, the professional asks and extracts it, mixes in their own knowledge, and delivers an artifact. If that pipeline can be replaced by AI, the economic impact is bound to be enormous. From the client's perspective: if something that used to cost $150 an hour with a professional can be handled for $20 a month? Of course the pressure to substitute is going to be immense.

As someone who also claims to be a professional, let me get to "so what am I supposed to do about it?" Where do people who've made a living all their lives by being good at asking run to now?

The fortunate part is that, for now, the `ask` tool is still heavily determined by the input of the person doing the asking. No matter how good the translator, it can't produce a masterpiece from a garbage manuscript. If the resolution of your question is low, the output that comes back is blurry. Conversely, for someone who knows clearly what they want and can ask precisely, AI works as tremendous leverage. There will definitely be a gap here for a while. A gap that arises from abstraction and the resolution of your questions. And for a while, whoever claims the widest share of that gap will be the recognized expert. What comes after that, I don't know yet.

Take developers as an example. Your team lead or a C-level exec casually tosses out an idea: "what if we tried it like this?" Here's how a genuinely good developer moves. First, they build a macro-level understanding of _why_ it should be done. What it means for the business, what value it can deliver to users. Next, they sketch a technical approach for _how_ it should be done. And they don't stop there — they consider broadly what the edge cases might be and what the real implementation risks are. They have to be able to communicate while holding this entire scope in their head.

And beyond that, they have to be able to propose before being asked. With all of the scope above already understood, they surface creative ideas the other person hadn't even considered. You have to be the person who says "what if we tried this?" first, not the one waiting to hear it.

Put plainly (and harshly): you have to become highly proactive and smart. And there's a bitter truth attached here — intelligence is relative. The gaps in capability and compensation caused by differences in intelligence are expected to widen even more radically going forward. A kind of rich-get-richer dynamic for intelligence.

There are hopeful angles too. Intelligence obviously has a genetic component, but the leverage from steadily accumulating knowledge through learning and curiosity — and the development of intelligence itself that happens along the way — is greater than it has ever been. Simply put, read and understand a lot of text, whether books or anything else. Reduced to its most boring form: keep _studying_. (Not the "studying stocks" kind.)

There's news that philosophy applications are rising worldwide these days, and I think those are very smart students. Anyone who has read the classics and seriously engaged in philosophical reasoning knows how purely, head-splittingly taxing "philosophy" is as a discipline, how much energy it sometimes takes just to _read_ a few lines of text, and how tangibly you feel a kind of brain growth once you've made it through that painful process.

The conclusion is simple. You have to get smarter. And in the era ahead, the reward for being smart will be far greater than it was in the era before.
